The Maori

The Maori are the indigenous people of New Zealand, a country they call Aotearoa. Their ancestors sailed across the Pacific Ocean in canoes and settled the islands around 1200 AD. Today, Maori make up about 15% of New Zealand's population.

When Europeans arrived in the early 1800s, relations were often difficult. In 1840, the British and many Maori chiefs signed the Treaty of Waitangi, which brought New Zealand under British law. Wars and land confiscation followed, and Maori lost much of their territory.

Maori culture remains a strong part of New Zealand life today. Te Reo Maori, the Maori language, has been an official language since 1985. The haka, a chanted dance with powerful movements, is used for greetings, celebrations and to challenge opponents — the All Blacks rugby team has performed it before matches since 1905. Seven seats in the New Zealand parliament are reserved for Maori representatives.

Aboriginal Australians

Aboriginal peoples have lived in Australia for at least 45,000–50,000 years, making them one of the oldest continuous cultures in the world. When the British began colonising Australia in 1788, life changed dramatically: many Aboriginal people died from violence and from diseases brought by settlers.

From the early 1900s until as late as the 1970s, thousands of Aboriginal children were forcibly taken from their families and placed in institutions or with white families — a policy that created what is now known as the Stolen Generations. The stated goal was to make the children adopt white Australian culture. The 1997 "Bringing Them Home" report found that at least 100,000 children had been removed.

On 13 February 2008, Prime Minister Kevin Rudd gave a formal apology to the Stolen Generations. Today, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples make up around 3.3% of Australia's population, and many still face lower life expectancy, higher unemployment and other inequalities linked to this history.

Easy Text: Australia's People

Aboriginal people are the first people of Australia. They lived there for about 40,000 years before Europeans came. When Europeans arrived, life became very hard for Aboriginal people. Many were killed, and many became sick from new diseases. Later, many Aboriginal children were taken away from their parents and raised in white families. This was very wrong. In 2008, Australia's prime minister said sorry for this. Today, Aboriginal people still often have less money and shorter lives than other Australians. But their culture — like the didjeridoo and the sacred place Uluru — is still important and alive today.

Native Americans

Long before Europeans arrived, an estimated 2.5 million Native Americans lived across North America, with rich cultures passed down through oral tradition — storytelling, songs, legends and prophecy — rather than written records. By the mid-1800s, disease, violence and greed had reduced this population to roughly 50,000.

Native American nations generally saw land differently from European settlers: rather than owning it, they saw themselves as caretakers for future generations. This did not stop the U.S. government from taking land by force. The Indian Removal Act of 1830 led to the forced relocation of five tribes and over 100,000 Native Americans to modern-day Oklahoma. The Cherokee's journey west, known as the Trail of Tears, cost thousands of lives.

Native Americans did not gain full U.S. citizenship until 1924. Today, about 2.9 million Native Americans belong to 562 federally recognised tribes, such as the Navajo, Cherokee and Sioux. About half live on reservations, which have their own governments but remain under federal oversight — leading many to argue that true sovereignty is still not fully recognised.
